# Working with Vulnerable Populations

## Research & Learn

### 1. Who are considered vulnerable populations, and what challenges might they face in digital spaces?

Vulnerable populations include people who may face additional challenges because of disabilities, mental health conditions, age, financial difficulties, or limited access to technology. This can include people with ADHD, Autism, learning disabilities, and other conditions that affect daily activities. In digital spaces, some people may struggle with complicated navigation, too much information, distracting notifications, or unclear instructions. For example, someone with ADHD might find it difficult to stay focused when an application has too many features, while an autistic person might find unexpected changes or excessive animations overwhelming. I believe technology should make people's lives easier rather than create additional stress.

### 2. What ethical considerations are important when designing or working with neurodivergent individuals?

When designing for neurodivergent individuals, we should respect their needs, preferences, privacy, and independence.

Some important considerations include:

- Keep things simple: Avoid complicated layouts and unnecessary distractions.
- Respect sensory needs: Reduce excessive animations, bright colours, and unexpected sounds.
- Protect privacy: Handle personal information carefully and give users control over their data.
- Provide flexibility: Allow users to customise notifications, reminders, and settings.
- Avoid judgment: Use encouraging language rather than making users feel guilty when they cannot complete a task.

Everyone experiences ADHD and Autism differently, so we should avoid assuming that one design will work for everyone.

### 3. How can you make interactions and content more accessible for people with ADHD or Autism?

I think the best approach is to make everything clear, simple, and predictable. For example, instead of showing users a long list of tasks, Focus Bear could help them concentrate on one task at a time. We can also use simple instructions, consistent navigation, clear buttons, and optional reminders. As someone working in QA, I would also check whether users can easily understand error messages, complete tasks without confusion, and adjust settings according to their needs. The goal should be to help users feel comfortable and confident while using the application.

### 4. How can we support neurodivergent team members in a professional setting?

I believe a supportive workplace starts with understanding that everyone communicates and works differently. We can support neurodivergent teammates by communicating clearly, providing written instructions when helpful, allowing flexible working styles, and respecting individual preferences. For example, some team members might prefer written communication instead of unexpected meetings, while others may need additional time to process information. I would also avoid making assumptions and encourage open communication so everyone feels comfortable asking questions or requesting support.

## Reflection

### 1. How can you adjust your communication style to be more inclusive of neurodivergent users and teammates?

I can improve my communication by using simple language, giving clear instructions, and avoiding unnecessary technical terms. Instead of explaining several things at once, I would break information into smaller steps and make sure the other person has enough time to understand. I would also be patient, listen carefully, and ask whether they prefer written instructions or verbal explanations. Most importantly, I would respect everyone's communication preferences rather than expecting everyone to communicate in the same way.

### 2. What are some common UX or communication pitfalls that might make Focus Bear less accessible or supportive?

Some common issues include:

- Too many notifications that interrupt concentration.
- Complicated navigation that makes it difficult to find features.
- Long instructions that require too much reading.
- Unexpected sounds, animations, or pop-ups.
- Unclear error messages that leave users confused.
- Negative messages that make users feel guilty for missing tasks.
- Limited options for personalising the application.

For example, showing a message like "You failed to complete your tasks today" might discourage someone who is already struggling with motivation. A more supportive message would be, "That's okay. You can try again when you're ready."

### 3. What is one practical change you can make in your work to better support vulnerable populations?

As a QA Engineer, one practical change I can make is to include accessibility checks in my regular testing process. For example, when testing Focus Bear, I would check whether instructions are easy to understand, navigation is consistent, and notifications can be customised. I would also pay attention to situations where users might feel confused, frustrated, or overwhelmed. I believe testing should not only focus on whether a feature works correctly but also on whether people can use it comfortably.

## Task

### 1. First-person account from someone with ADHD or Autism about productivity tools

One useful first-person perspective to explore is from Jessica McCabe, who shares her experiences of living with ADHD through [How to ADHD](https://www.youtube.com/@HowtoADHD).

Her content discusses challenges such as getting started on tasks, managing time, remembering responsibilities, and using strategies to make everyday activities more manageable. A key lesson I take from these experiences is that productivity tools should support people instead of making them feel pressured to be productive all the time. For someone with ADHD, even starting a small task can sometimes feel difficult. A tool that provides too many reminders or complicated features might make the situation worse. Reference for further reading or viewing: How to ADHD. This is a general reflection on the creator's shared experiences, not a review of one specific video.

### 2. Identify one design or communication improvement that could make Focus Bear more accessible.

One improvement I would suggest is introducing a simple, optional one-task-at-a-time mode. Instead of displaying multiple tasks and reminders, Focus Bear could show the user's current task with a clear button to start, pause, or complete it. This could help reduce distractions and make it easier for users to focus on what they need to do next. I would also make sure users can turn this feature on or off based on their preferences.

### 3. Practice writing a clear, patient, and supportive response to a hypothetical user struggling with executive functioning.

Scenario: A user says, "I have so many tasks to complete, but I don't know where to start. I feel overwhelmed and frustrated."

My response: I understand that having too many tasks at once can feel overwhelming, especially when you're not sure where to begin. You don't have to complete everything right now. Maybe we can start with just one small task that feels manageable. You can take your time, and it's okay to take breaks when you need them. Even making a little progress is a good start. Would you like some help breaking your tasks into smaller steps? I chose this response because it is patient, encouraging, and doesn't put pressure on the user. It acknowledges their feelings while offering practical support without making assumptions about what they can or cannot do.

## Final Reflection

This task helped me understand that accessibility is not just about making an application easy to use. It is also about making people feel respected, understood, and supported. As a QA Engineer at Focus Bear, I want to consider the experiences of neurodivergent users while testing features, identifying usability issues, and suggesting improvements. My biggest takeaway is that good software should adapt to people's needs rather than expecting people to adapt to the software.
